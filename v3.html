Bobroff Capital · 6 August 2026

Where we are going inside MSP

Direction after three days at ChannelCon 2026, San Diego. Method, sources and working detail sit in the analysis layer; the economics are a separate model.
Managed services looks like a fragmented industry, but the distance between a weak provider and a strong one is half the gross margin and half the engineer utilisation — and that distance is operational. It is made of systems, and systems move between companies.

So what we buy is the gap, not market share: thirteen small providers serving broker-dealers and registered investment advisers in the United States, migrated onto one delivery platform, which we build on a first anchor asset before we buy the second.
1 · The gap

The industry runs at two speeds, and a buyer for the assembled group already exists

Around 40,000 to 50,000 providers operate in the United States and none holds a meaningful share. The full-day financial masterclass at ChannelCon put the peer-group data on screen, and it is the most useful thing we brought back.

The mechanics behind those rows hold together. The bottom quartile pushes three and a half times the ticket volume through each engineer and gets a fifteen-hour first response for it, against forty-six minutes for the profitable cohort — efficiency and service quality fail together. Churn accounts for roughly three quarters of the growth gap. The shrinking cohort spends 0.4% of revenue on winning work; the growing one spends 1.5%.

The independent index says the same from another angle: investment-class providers have held 19%+ adjusted EBITDA for six consecutive years, and top-quartile firms earn about 2.5 times the EBITDA percentage of their median peers, attributed to operational maturity. The leading figures come from firms that are mostly small, so none of this is about size.

And the exit is not hypothetical. Deal volume has more than doubled in three years, two thirds of it with private-equity money behind it, and thirty to fifty regional platforms are assembling right now. Whoever finishes a clean group of this kind has a queue of buyers.

2 · What the gap is made of

Since we buy the gap, it is worth being exact about the systems that close it

Our own operating work, built against a live client base, puts numbers on the delivery model. The platform absorbs 55% of ticket volume, a remote engineer handles 35%, and 10% needs someone on site — which for a mid-market client is one to four visits a year.

That last line is the one that changes what kind of business this is. If coverage were a staffing problem, a roll-up would mean hiring engineers in every state we buy into. It is not. It is a software and go-to-market problem, and the physical presence is a contract with a field network. That is why one platform can serve providers in states where we have nobody.

3 · Where the gap is worth most

The same systems earn far more where the client is buying evidence for a regulator

An MSP runs another company's IT, and its «vertical» is the industry its clients are in. Fifty-eight per cent of US providers now concentrate on at least one, against thirty-nine in 2021, and it pays: specialists price at $250–350 per seat against $100–120 and carry roughly 23% more margin. The systems are the same everywhere; what differs is what a client will pay to have them.

4 · What we buy

Six marks describe the company, and two of them remove half the field before geography is even considered

The vertical decides what the target looks like. A provider that serves regulated financial firms sells coverage, evidence and advice — and a provider whose revenue is mostly resold vendor subscriptions sells none of those, whatever its margin looks like on paper.

5 · Where we find them

There is no marketplace for these companies, so the channel is a small number of rooms

Managed services has no dedicated event for selling a business. Deal rooms exist only as tracks inside owner conferences, and the one M&A-specific show stopped running in 2024. That leaves a short list, and it is short enough to name.

6 · Who else is here

The bottom of the market is open because the funded consolidators do not come down this far

Every consolidator publishes a floor, and the floors are what leave us room. Evergreen buys at $3M of revenue and above. The integrator platforms start at $2M of EBITDA. In the lower half of our pool they are simply not bidding, and the number of contenders for sub-$5M assets has fallen from four or five in 2023 to two or three now.

So the opening is real and it is timed. Nobody funded is competing for the assets we want today. Two platforms with working automation are buying upward toward that same population, and their advantage compounds with every provider they migrate. This is the argument for moving in the next two quarters instead of the next four.

7 · The sequence, and the next months

The platform is assembled on a first asset, and from the second deal migration is the integration

Every entry posture in this market has a hard precondition — permanent capital, a sponsor, a credit line, or a platform that already works. We do not have a platform yet, which settles the order of operations. The first provider we buy, together with the managed-services operation we already run, is where it gets built. From the second acquisition onward, moving a company onto that platform is the integration, which is what makes three deals a year possible where thirteen at once is not.

The next months. Three things run in parallel. We name the pool firm by firm and open conversations directly with owners. We put ourselves in the October and November rooms, and start the sponsorship or referral route into the peer groups, which is a season of work before it yields anything. And we buy the anchor and begin building the platform on it and on our own operation.

What would tell us the direction is wrong. Owners in this slice refuse centralised terms and sell only to buyers who leave them alone. Or a bought company's margin does not move over two or three quarters, which would mean the systems do not transfer the way the peer-group data implies. Or the funded platforms come down into our cheque size faster than we assemble, and the opening closes before we are through it.

The delivery model, the filter method and the competitive detail sit in the analysis layer. The economics — entry pricing, capital required and return — are worked in the financial model and are not repeated here.
