Why the runner-up was selected

Home Care scored 8.73 against MSP at 8.69 — a gap inside the noise of a qualitative frame. The screen weights AI compressibility at 0.12, right for ranking industries in the abstract, low for a buyer whose only durable edge is deployment. At 0.25 the order inverts — MSP 8.83, Home Care 8.58, break-even near 0.15. The substantive difference behind the arithmetic: in home care labour is the service and Medicaid sets the rate, so neither margin nor price is ours to move. Home Care stays a listening post rather than a rejection.

The two verticals that scored well and are still unbuyable. Insurance brokerage ranks third on fundamentals with PE-headroom of 3 — every asset contested by capital that arrived twenty years ago. Accounting fails from the opposite end with AI-compressibility of 2: the service itself is absorbed inside 24–36 months, so the buyer acquires a melting asset. A high score is not a decision, and the binding constraint differs by vertical.

Node 02

Vertical

Specialisation is priced, not positioned — but the record contains two different theories of what to specialise in, and they lead to different companies.

The question, and the competing framings of it

An MSP can specialise along two axes, and the conference argued both. Framing A — client industry: pick the vertical, learn its compliance language, become the firm that says «all we do is what you do». Framing B — mandated workflow: ignore the industry, find a workflow a regulator forces a business to prove it performs, automate it once, then sell that automation to every MSP carrying the same obligation. The two framings differ in what is scarce. A treats domain reputation as the moat; B treats the compliance mandate as the moat and is indifferent to the industry attached to it.

Framing B was argued against this thesis directly. A solution provider challenged the premise of the search itself — that leverage comes not from finding the perfect market but from exploiting the gap between a business's belief in its resilience and the evidence of its recovery capability, which regulation forces into the open. On that view, choosing the industry first is optimising the wrong variable. Framing B is not adopted here, and Node 05 explains why: it is a product strategy, and it presupposes a channel we do not yet have.

Why financial services, given that it is not the densest option

Healthcare and legal have the better economics on the evidence — the density plays are proven and repeatable, which is precisely why they are already occupied. Selecting them means buying our way into a room where the incumbent has a twenty-year head start and no reason to sell cheaply. Financial services is chosen on a different logic: it is the only vertical where the acquirer brings something the incumbent does not, because the team has already built software for trading firms and can speak the risk language without hiring it in. That is the whole of the differentiation, and it should be stated as narrowly as that.

The CMMC overlay has a date on it, and the date is inside the planning horizon. The DoD published the final DFARS rule on 10 September 2025; Phase 1 enforcement began 10 November 2025; Phase 2 — mandatory third-party certification across a broader set of contracts — begins 10 November 2026, roughly three months from this document. Against 350,000 suppliers in the defence industrial base, surveyed readiness has fallen rather than risen: 1% of contractors are fully prepared, down from 4% in 2025 and 8% in 2023. Analysts expect 33,000–44,000 firms to leave defence work entirely because compliance costs exceed the value of the contracts. That is a demand shock with a fixed date, a measurable shortfall and a priced service attached — and it is the clearest link between the vertical choice here and the security track in Node 05.

CMMC sits on top rather than beside. It is the highest-priced obligation in the record and it is a certification rather than a reputation — meaning it can be acquired on a schedule rather than earned over years. It is treated here as an overlay to be added to acquired assets, not as a vertical to be entered on its own.

Segment — and a correction to the working brief

Not enterprise, which is bidding against very large firms. Not SMB, whose decisions are «largely driven on cost». The mid-market outsources by preference, resists headcount and is willing to spend.

The range circulating internally is 15–500 employees. The source said 50–500. The 15–50 band is the price-driven SMB the same expert ruled out; carrying it into the ICP imports the segment we were warned against and drags the entry multiple with it.

What this rests on, and what would change it

It rests on one assumption doing all the work: that domain credibility transfers from crypto trading software to mid-market financial-services IT. Adjacent is not the same as inside, and the claim is untested against a buyer in that segment. Two signals would overturn the choice. If the first ten conversations in financial services do not convert faster than a generalist pitch, the edge is imaginary and the vertical is arbitrary — at which point healthcare density becomes the better bet despite the incumbents. And if CMMC engagements price above the vertical's own premium, the obligation is worth more than the industry, which is Framing B winning on evidence and a different company.

Evidence: ChannelCon 2026 — 22-year Arizona MSP owner (law firms and compliance-based businesses); RMM/ticketing product lead on PCI and HIPAA specialisation; density cases in Dallas and Atlanta; the solution-provider challenge to vertical-first framing; consultation on mid-market financial services, 50–500 employees. Premium figures: CMMC $4–8K per client per month; compliance focus +20–40% revenue and +3–5 pp EBITDA per Node 01.

The arithmetic was done out loud at the conference. Working back from a $500M outcome at a 12× exit: «if I take five hundred divided by twelve, I need forty-one EBITDA» — and then the consequence, that assembling it out of small firms at 5–8× means «almost two hundred million», which is why a fund enters the picture at all.

Set against the consolidator actually operating in this market: Evergreen Services Group closed 47 acquisitions in 2025 alone — 33 of them MSPs — and targets 30–40 MSPs in 2026. Private equity appeared in 69% of disclosed MSP deals in 2025 across 169 transactions. The required rate is therefore not the constraint it first appears to be — twelve a year is roughly a third of what the market leader executes — but that is the less comfortable reading, not the more comfortable one.

The competition is not other bidders at the auction; it is a machine running three times our speed with committed capital behind it. Every asset in the target band is inside Evergreen's stated 2026 mandate. Being below the PE radar is a statement about deal size, not about attention — 69% PE participation says the radar covers the band. What remains defensible is being faster to a specific owner in a specific vertical, which is the case Node 09 has to carry.

Is there enough supply

No. Supply is not the binding constraint, and it is worth stating plainly so that effort is not spent widening the funnel. Against ~40–45K US providers with median revenue of $2–5M and median EBITDA near 13%, the majority of the market sits inside the $150–850K band by construction. Applying a 60%+ recurring filter removes roughly half. What remains is measured in thousands against a requirement measured in dozens.

The binding constraint is integration throughput. Sourcing is a network problem with a known solution — peer groups hold ~130 owners across 14–15 groups and «almost all of them want to sell at some point», and access there is brokered rather than bought. Absorbing twelve companies a year without losing the margin uplift is an operating problem with no solution in the record. That is where the organisational build should be pointed, and it is the reason the integration lead is named before the M&A lead.

The first deal is deliberately the exception

It has to be large enough to give the platform a footprint the smaller ones fold into: revenue $2–3M, net 15–20%, price $3–3.5M, with several consecutive years of that profit. Fastest close on record from the same source is 90 days; with cash ready and counsel in place, a month is possible.

Two clauses decide the multiple, and one of them is worth more than the discount

Agreements must be assignable — without that clause a sale falls to state law, which differs everywhere. And a three-year agreement with a 90-day exit is a month-to-month agreement: «your business is worth about half the multiple you think it is». One-year terms instead of three cost roughly 1.5× of multiple — larger than the entire sub-radar discount, and available for the price of a renewal negotiation rather than an acquisition.

Waiting is the most expensive option on the table. From the buyer's side of a live case: $2M offered in the autumn; the owner waited, lost three key contracts, and the January offer was $1.1M — which closed. Six months of hesitation cost nearly a million dollars.

The window is closing while the thesis is being written

Transactions ran 435 → 675 → 782 → 1,000+ across 2022–2025, average deal size doubled from $2.5M to $5.0M, and average enterprise value rose ~15% in a single year. Consolidation is outpacing market growth, which means the same assets are being bid by more capital each year. Entry multiples are the thing that moves first. The plan assumes 5–6× at entry; that assumption has a shelf life, and the sub-radar band is the last place it survives — which is an argument for speed rather than for a wider mandate.

A product cannot exist before the channel that proves it

A product does not exist before the channel does. Jeff McHugh, solution provider: automate one critical, regulator-mandated workflow for one MSP in a regulated industry — “and suddenly the software you just wrote becomes your product.
