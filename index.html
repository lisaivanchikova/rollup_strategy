MSP roll-up — the argument in three steps

Every figure, source and alternative considered is in the full analysis. This page is the chain of reasoning, not a summary of it.
The gap between a well-run managed service provider and a badly-run one is operational — half the gross margin, half the utilisation, three and a half times the ticket load per engineer — and operational gaps transfer, which is what makes this industry consolidatable rather than merely fragmented. We propose to buy thirteen small providers serving mid-market financial firms, standardise them onto one delivery platform and reach $100M of enterprise value in five years — and what decides whether that works is not finding deals but the capacity to integrate them.
Step 1 · The premise

Fragmented industries are usually bad places to consolidate. This one is the exception, and the exhibit says why

Roughly 40,000 to 50,000 providers operate in the United States and none holds a meaningful share. On the standard reading that is a fragmented industry — many ways to compete, little advantage available to any of them — and the usual conclusion is that buying more of it achieves nothing.

The operating data contradicts that, and everything below depends on it.

This is a narrow claim and worth stating precisely. We are not betting that bigger providers are better — the leading figures above come from firms that are mostly small. We are betting that the distance between quartiles is made of things a competent operator installs. If instead it were driven by market, client mix or geography, none of it would transfer, and the strategy would collapse into buying revenue at a multiple.

If the gap is installable, the question becomes which firms to install it in — and that is a narrowing problem, because most of the 40,000 are the wrong ones.
Step 2 · What we buy

Seven filters cut 40,000 providers to a pool in the low hundreds — small enough that it has to be enumerated by name rather than marketed to

An MSP runs another company's IT. Its «vertical» is the industry its clients are in — a financial-services provider serves banks, brokers and advisers; a healthcare one serves clinics and dental practices. Fifty-eight per cent of US providers now concentrate on at least one, against thirty-nine in 2021, and the concentration pays: specialists price at $250–350 per seat against $100–120 and carry roughly 23% more margin. That premium is the second reason to narrow, on top of the first.

Which verticals exist inside MSP, and what happened to each

Financial services is chosen over healthcare on room rather than on quality. Healthcare is larger and grows faster, and it is the most consolidated slice in the market — its vertical concentration went from 18% to 31% in a single year, with financial services second and moving the same way. What actually selects ours is transferable domain knowledge from crypto-trading software, and that is a claim about ourselves that has never been tested in this market. The screen was deliberately run with that criterion weighted at 0.06 so the answer would not depend on it; the top of the board holds without it.

A pool of seventy to two hundred firms can be bought. But buying them means integrating them — and integration, not sourcing, is what kills roll-ups.
Step 3 · How we enter

Over 70% of integrations miss what was modelled, and deals with more than two add-ons returned less than none — so the entry question is what we can integrate, not what we can buy

Private-equity deals carrying more than two add-ons returned 19.9% against 23.1% for standalone buyouts. More acquisitions returned less. The binding constraint is integration throughput, and it is built over repeated deals rather than bought — which reframes the field below. The positions are not degrees of risk appetite; they are entry requirements.

Two of the four are closed to us by what they demand before the first deal: permanent capital in one case, a private-equity sponsor in the other — and the model that never sells cannot serve a founder exit in any case. The fourth, replacing the acquired operation outright, is where the largest prize sits and requires a working platform to exist beforehand. The middle position was misread on our side: it was taken to mean leaving acquired firms alone, whereas that operator standardises everything from day one and says so publicly. There are two live positions, not three.

Which produces the entry, and it is a sequence rather than a choice. Buy one provider at $3–4M of revenue and 5–6× adjusted EBITDA. Build the delivery platform on it and on the operation we already run. From the second deal onward, migration onto that platform is the integration — which is how the ambition of rebuilding gets executed at a throughput of three a year instead of thirteen at once. A larger anchor is not a judgement call: at $10M of revenue it costs $18M, sixty per cent of the budget for a quarter of the target. We cannot buy a platform; we can only assemble one.

The ask is one signed LOI — not a round, not a partner, not another quarter of research. It is the only next step that resolves more than one open question at once: it prices our client-acquisition cost, tests the integration claim, gives a lender a borrowing base, and shows whether small firms in this slice transact at 4–5× or at 6×, which is the single assumption the arbitrage rests on.

The kill criterion is equally small. A hundred contacts and no LOI across two quarters means sellers will not transact at our price — and it is the entry model that failed, not the vertical.

Three things this argument does not establish: that we can integrate, which the outside record says is what kills roll-ups; demand for the software layer beyond our own portfolio; and the vertical on market data rather than on a claim about ourselves.
