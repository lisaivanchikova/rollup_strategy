Two practitioners, thirty-seven deals between them, converge on the same line

Kevin Damghani, seven deals, IT Partners: target profile is 60%+ recurring revenue and $150–850K EBITDA — “generally under a million, because PE gets excited about a million or more.”

Steve, thirty deals, peer-group operator: “I would go even smaller, because I want to be below the radar of all the PE money.” The line sits near $500K. The reason is arithmetic — “I want companies we can get at five or six times, not the ones we have to take to seven, eight, nine to compete with another buyer.”

The first deal is the exception

It has to be larger, to give the platform a footprint the smaller ones can be folded into: revenue $2–3M, net profit 15–20%, price $3–3.5M, several consecutive years of that profit. Fastest close on record from the same source: 90 days; with cash ready and counsel in place, a month is possible.

Two contract clauses decide the multiple. Agreements must be assignable — without that clause a sale falls to state law, which differs everywhere. And a three-year agreement with a 90-day exit is a month-to-month agreement: “your business is worth about half the multiple you think it is.” One-year terms instead of three cost roughly 1.5× of multiple.
Waiting is the most expensive option on the table. A live case from the buyer’s side: $2M offered in the autumn; the owner waited and lost three key contracts; the January offer was $1.1M, and it closed at that. Six months of hesitation cost nearly a million dollars.

A product cannot exist before the channel that proves it

A product does not exist before the channel does. Jeff McHugh, solution provider: automate one critical, regulator-mandated workflow for one MSP in a regulated industry — “and suddenly the software you just wrote becomes your product. You turn around and sell it to MSP after MSP after MSP.” Distribution runs through the RMM vendors — Kaseya, ConnectWise, NinjaOne — “pick one, become friends.”

“First you buy the client base, then you build software on top of it… I don’t believe in building software and then running around trying to push it onto every MSP — that just turns us back into SaaS.” — Nicolas, 4 August

The same direction is priced into the scorecard: the highest multiples go to an 80% MRR / 20% hardware-and-projects mix on three-year assignable agreements. Damghani

What the choice rests on. That the channel can be built at all. Today the MSP leg is seven people and one anchor client at 25 seats.

Two axes are closed by evidence; the simulation only stress-tests the third

Axes A and B are settled by evidence, so the cube collapses from eight branches to two: offshore-led and onshore-led, both entered below the PE radar with the channel built before the product. The simulation is therefore not a search for the winning branch — it is a stress test of one question: at what integration churn and what utilisation ramp does the offshore-led branch stop paying?

Inputs are known from the benchmark set and belong in the financial model, not here: entry multiple 4–6×, exit 8–10× (15–16× at $40M+), gross margin 40% → 51.2% leading, monthly churn 0.5% at leading versus 1.3% at the bottom quartile, utilisation 61% → 70%.

Held in the financial model. The Monte-Carlo run lives in the financial model, which is maintained separately. This node carries the branch structure and the question; the probabilities attach when the model is rebased on the benchmark set above.

Sell to the CEO, not to the IT director

Not the IT director. From a 30-year specialist: for small and mid-sized businesses, “CTOs and CIOs don’t know a lot… it never ceases to surprise me how little they know.” His own conclusion on what he would change: “sell to the CEO and not the CIO — I still haven’t cracked that nut.” The craft is selling a complex technical proposition to a non-technical buyer.

Sales and marketing as a share of revenue: bottom quartile 0.4%, leading peer group 1.5%, top quartile 2.6%. The bottom quartile is also the one losing half a point of recurring revenue every month.
