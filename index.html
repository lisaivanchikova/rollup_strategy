Node 00

Narrowing

This document is a set of cuts, not a plan. Everything below removes a degree of freedom — and the newest constraint contradicts the archetype chosen in May, which is the first thing to settle.

What was already decided, and on what basis

The May work ran the full field: fourteen archetypes available to an operator holding three enablers — US market access, SMB acquisition capability, AI deployment capability — scored on eight parameters against founder cash-out at exit. Its honest finding was that no archetype scores in the top quartile on all eight; every game carries a structural trade-off. The combined utility of the three enablers concentrated in one archetype, and the posture memo fixed it as AI-Native Operating Holdco with paid pre-acquisition pilots, with the two runner-up paths failing four of five structural tests.

Both readings cannot hold. Either the AI-native archetype stands and the entry model is the radical right-hand column — in which case the target can be argued upward and the risk is concentration. Or the middle path is taken as the realistic entry — in which case the May archetype is diluted at the moment of purchase, because an owner who keeps running his firm is not an operating company being rebuilt around AI. This is the single decision that orders everything downstream. It is resolved in Node 04, and it resolves in neither direction: the middle column below is a description of the offer, not of the operating model — the platform it describes is centralised, and the archetype survives by being sequenced rather than by winning the argument.

The target, as stated

Not open any longer, and the two figures are not variants of one ambition. $100M — «visible, understandable, viable, but a serious game: bite down and grind». $1B — «I don't see any model for it anywhere near», with the observation that the pull toward that figure comes from the fund side. The billion, if it exists, sits in the right-hand column above. This document is written for the hundred and marks where the billion would have to come from.

Four questions, in the order they get decided: which vertical survives the test in Node 04 rather than intuition · which entry quadrant wins in Node 06 · where on the integration scale we actually stand, Node 07 · onshore or offshore. Why the runner-up was selected

Home Care scored 8.73 against MSP at 8.69 — a gap inside the noise of a qualitative frame. The screen weights AI compressibility at 0.12, right for ranking industries in the abstract, low for a buyer whose only durable edge is deployment. At 0.25 the order inverts — MSP 8.83, Home Care 8.58, break-even near 0.15. The substantive difference behind the arithmetic: in home care labour is the service and Medicaid sets the rate, so neither margin nor price is ours to move. Home Care stays a listening post rather than a rejection.

The two verticals that scored well and are still unbuyable. Insurance brokerage ranks third on fundamentals with PE-headroom of 3 — every asset contested by capital that arrived twenty years ago. Node 02

Segment

Specialisation is not a marketing posture, it is a 23% margin difference and a 2.5× difference in price per seat. The axis of specialisation is a real choice; the middle of it is not available.

The question, and the rule that constrains the answer

Two mutually exclusive axes of specialisation exist, both observed at the conference in the same room. By client industry — learn one sector's language and compliance load, build the reputation, «walk into any vertical because everyone there knows me». By service — a single capability sold industry-agnostically; the working example was an operator doing nothing but database administration. Two operators, two opposite theories, both viable.

The constraint is on the middle, and it is stated flatly. «I don't think the middle options — I'm not here, not there, undecided — are good or fit. It means we simply haven't done our homework.» Everything below therefore has to pick an axis, not blend them.

This settles the axis question in favour of industry specialisation for our case, and it does so on economics rather than preference. The service-specialist route is not refuted — it is simply the weaker version of the same moat, because a capability can be hired while a compliance record cannot. The counter-example from the conference makes the same point from the other side: the database-only operator sat at $750K of revenue after three decades — high margin, no growth engine, unable to win new clients.

What is actually being sold — the compliance stack

The vertical is not a marketing label; it is a defined set of obligations the client must satisfy and cannot satisfy alone. For US mid-market financial firms the stack overlaps and sometimes pulls in different directions: SEC Regulation S-P on detection, response and recovery from unauthorised access to customer data · FINRA Rules 3110 and 4370 on continuous system supervision and business continuity · FINRA 17a-4(f) on records · GLBA Safeguards Rule, enforced by the FTC across RIAs, advisers and lenders, requiring a written information security programme, a named qualified individual, encryption, MFA, access controls and vendor oversight · plus SOX, CFTC, FDIC and FFIEC expectations, and GDPR or MiFID II on cross-border work.

The sentence that makes this a moat rather than a feature list: FINRA examinations, SEC cybersecurity requirements and GLBA audits require documentation that a generic MSP cannot produce. The barrier is evidentiary, not technical — which is why it converts into $250–350 per seat instead of $100–120, and why it cannot be undercut by a cheaper generalist.

Segment boundaries

Mid-market: not enterprise, which means bidding against very large firms; not SMB, whose decisions are «largely driven on cost». The mid-market outsources by preference, resists headcount and is willing to spend.

The range circulating internally is 15–500 employees. The source said 50–500, and the discrepancy was introduced in the room rather than in the record. The veteran gave the range as fifty to five hundred; it was repeated back as fifteen within the same exchange and went uncorrected, and the lower figure has travelled through the internal brief ever since. The 15–50 band is the price-driven SMB the same expert ruled out — carrying it into the ICP imports the segment we were warned against and drags the entry multiple with it.

The test this segment still has to pass

A vertical is not selected until five things close on each other: the job → the vertical → the segment → the solution → the cost of the acquisition channel, out of the four channels available, ending in unit economics that work. Any one of them failing means the segment is not ours regardless of how attractive the market looks. Two of the five are open. The cost of the channel is unknown because the channel has not been run — Node 09. And the solution is only half-specified: the compliance stack above says what the client needs, not what we uniquely deliver against it.

What would change this

If the first ten financial-services conversations do not convert measurably faster than a generalist pitch, the asserted edge is imaginary and the vertical becomes arbitrary — at which point healthcare's superior growth and exit premium outweigh the incumbents, and the reserve becomes the selection. If CMMC engagements price above the vertical premium, the obligation is worth more than the industry and the axis shifts from client industry to mandated workflow — a different company, described in Node 06. And if a competitor with the same crypto-adjacent background appears in the segment, the edge was never proprietary.

The California claim, tested

The warning received was that California has difficult legislation and a low concentration of MSP firms. Half of that is not supported. California is a recognised MSP cluster — San Francisco and Los Angeles both appear in every mapping of technical hubs, alongside New York, Austin, Dallas, Miami, Chicago and Seattle. On raw firm counts the state is not thin.

The other half is supported, and the mechanism is more useful than the claim. From the conference floor: a veteran solo operator in California who has deliberately refused to hire for years, describing the regulatory and administrative overhead of having employees in the state as a definitive, non-negotiable barrier, and choosing a lifestyle practice over expansion because of it. That is not one owner's temperament — it is the predictable response to the cost structure below.

The finding, stated properly. California does not lack MSPs. It lacks MSPs with employees — the only kind that can be acquired. Its labour regime raises the cost of the fifth employee enough that a rational owner stops at four, and a firm that never hired has no transferable operation to buy. Low supply of assets is the output of the legislation, not a separate fact about the state.

And it collides with the vertical. Node 02 selects mid-market financial services, where 7×24 coverage is a precondition rather than an upsell. Round-the-clock coverage means night shifts, on-call rotations and monitored breaks — precisely the three exposures California prices highest. The vertical and the state are not independent choices, and this pairing is the worst available.

What this changes

Two states, two different jobs. New York is the anchor — it is where the firm already operates and where the selected vertical is densest, so the first platform deal and the vertical proof happen here. Texas is the expansion market — it is the only state where cheap operations, the largest defence demand pool in the country and a real MSP cluster coincide, which makes it the natural home for the CMMC overlay and for the acquisitions that follow the anchor. California is excluded from the acquisition map and remains reachable through delivery from elsewhere if a client requires it.

A second-order point on standards. The industry has no universal standard and operates as a patchwork of state regulation — which is what makes geography a strategic variable at all. That is now being organised: GTIA and Texas A&M's Global Cybersecurity Research Institute have formed a Consortium for Responsible IT Services to define operational and technical standards, first meeting October 2026. If a self-regulatory standard lands, the state-by-state arbitrage this node depends on compresses. Worth tracking, not worth planning around yet.

What would change this

A New York cost-of-employment test coming out materially worse than assumed would move the anchor, not just the expansion — the state has its own scheduling and spread-of-hours rules that have not been priced here and should be before headcount scales. Evidence that Texas asset prices already carry a premium from consolidator activity would flip the expansion market to Florida. And a California asset arriving with 7×24 already staffed and compliant would be worth re-testing on its own merits — the exclusion is about the cost of building that operation, not about owning one that exists.

The provider side — and the gap that is itself a finding

Against 40–50K US providers, no published source counts how many specialise in financial services. Vertical specialists are a minority of the field, and financial services is one of the three verticals driving growth — but the population is unmapped. That absence cuts both ways: it means our own funnel has to be built by hand, and it means no consolidator has mapped this population either. An unmapped population is a defensible position only for whoever maps it first.

What is known is the purchase profile in provider terms: EBITDA $150–850K at a 13% median margin implies revenue of roughly $1.2–6.5M per target — which at $400–600K per client is an operator with somewhere between three and sixteen mid-market financial clients. That is a very small number of relationships, and it re-frames diligence: at this size, client concentration is not a risk factor to be scored, it is the defining characteristic of every asset we will look at. Node 03 flags concentration as the most common cause of multiple compression; here it is structural.

What the required deal count does to this profile

Node 02c sets the count: thirteen assets for $100M, thirty-eight for $300M, sixty-two for $500M. The profile below is written for the first of those. It does not survive the third unchanged — sixty-two acquisitions at two to three a year is arithmetically impossible, so the $500M version requires either larger targets, which means paying PE prices and losing the entry arbitrage, or a deal rate above anything a first-time acquirer has achieved. The profile and the target are not independent choices, and the target should be set from the achievable rate rather than the rate inferred from the target.

The competition, sized properly. Evergreen closed 47 acquisitions in 2025 — 33 of them MSPs — and targets 30–40 MSPs in 2026. Private equity appeared in 69% of disclosed MSP deals across 169 transactions. At under three deals a year we are not competing on volume with that machine; we are competing for specific owners in one vertical in two states, which is the only contest we can win. Being below the PE radar is a statement about deal size, not about attention: 69% participation says the radar covers the band.

Is there enough supply

No. Supply is not the binding constraint, and it is worth stating plainly so that effort is not spent widening the funnel. Against ~40–45K US providers with median revenue of $2–5M and median EBITDA near 13%, the majority of the market sits inside the $150–850K band by construction. Applying a 60%+ recurring filter removes roughly half. What remains is measured in thousands against a requirement measured in dozens.

The binding constraint is integration throughput. Sourcing is a network problem with a known solution — peer groups hold ~130 owners across 14–15 groups and «almost all of them want to sell at some point», and access there is brokered rather than bought. Absorbing twelve companies a year without losing the margin uplift is an operating problem with no solution in the record. That is where the organisational build should be pointed, and it is the reason the integration lead is named before the M&A lead.

The first deal is deliberately the exception

It has to be large enough to give the platform a footprint the smaller ones fold into: revenue $2–3M, net 15–20%, price $3–3.5M, with several consecutive years of that profit. Fastest close on record from the same source is 90 days; with cash ready and counsel in place, a month is possible.

Two clauses decide the multiple, and one of them is worth more than the discount

Agreements must be assignable — without that clause a sale falls to state law, which differs everywhere. And a three-year agreement with a 90-day exit is a month-to-month agreement: «your business is worth about half the multiple you think it is». One-year terms instead of three cost roughly 1.5× of multiple — larger than the entire sub-radar discount, and available for the price of a renewal negotiation rather than an acquisition.

Waiting is the most expensive option on the table. From the buyer's side of a live case: $2M offered in the autumn; the owner waited, lost three key contracts, and the January offer was $1.1M — which closed. Six months of hesitation cost nearly a million dollars.

The window is closing while the thesis is being written

Transactions ran 435 → 675 → 782 → 1,000+ across 2022–2025, average deal size doubled from $2.5M to $5.0M, and average enterprise value rose ~15% in a single year. Consolidation is outpacing market growth, which means the same assets are being bid by more capital each year. Entry multiples are the thing that moves first. The plan assumes 5–6× at entry; that assumption has a shelf life, and the sub-radar band is the last place it survives — which is an argument for speed rather than for a wider mandate.

The middle of the scale, as we described it, does not exist. The operator we modelled it on runs a centralised platform and says so from the stage — which leaves two real positions, and the outside data says the choice between them is a test of capability, not of appetite.

The correction: we placed ITPartners+ one position to the left of where it sits

Our internal reading was that this operator leaves the acquired firm alone — assess, keep the strengths, absorb the weak functions — and that a centralising turn is a hidden move some years later. Neither half survives contact with what he actually said.

The assessment is about the owner's job, not the firm's operations. The strengths kept and the weak functions absorbed are HR, finance and accounting lifted off the owner's plate, with the owner's remaining role chosen through the Delegate and Elevate exercise from EOS. The company itself goes to one team and one coat of paint from the outset. He states the contrast himself: his model is centralised, as against the decentralised platforms where the acquired MSP keeps its brand and the owner becomes a general manager.

So there is no pyramid to flip at year five — the centralisation is published on day one, and it is what the owners are buying into. What we read as concealment is the offer working as designed: owners who will not sell for cash will merge for a multiple they cannot reach alone. The mechanics of that offer are Node 06; what matters here is that the middle we thought we had found is a centralised platform with a partnership wrapper, and the scale therefore has two live positions, not three.

What the five-year clock actually is

The facility matures in five years and requires recapitalisation; the group's plan is to have grown into an attractive acquisition target for private equity inside that window, and it is roughly eighteen months in. The owners' promised second exit and the lender's maturity date are the same event. That is not a trick, it is the structure — and it carries one consequence we cannot copy without inheriting: this posture is not permanent capital. It runs to a fixed date, and everything about the integration pace is set by that date rather than by what the acquired firms can absorb.

The choice, and why not the runner-up

Centralised platform, with the AI layer built on our own operation first and every subsequent acquisition migrated onto it. The decentralised posture is rejected on the criterion rather than on taste: a model whose defining promise is that it never sells cannot serve a founder cash-out, and the version that does sell requires the sponsor whose absence is the point of the exercise.

The rebuild is not rejected — it is put in sequence. Industry commentary describes the same move we scored at the right edge: buy the client base, migrate it onto the delivery platform, capture the margin difference. The actors doing it already had the platform. We do not, and building it and using it on thirteen assets at once is the specific failure the IRR data describes.

This resolves the contradiction opened in Node 00, and it resolves it by sequencing rather than by choosing a side. The May archetype is not diluted at the moment of purchase — it is deferred by exactly one asset. The AI-native operating model gets built inside the operation we already run and the first firm we buy; from deal two onward, migration onto that platform is the integration, which is why the centralised posture and the archetype are the same programme rather than opposites. It also reframes our own MSP leg, currently ~$150K a year and named in Node 00 as the weak point: it is not a pilot, it is where the platform gets built before it is expensive to get wrong.

What this rests on, and what would break it

It rests on three things, none yet demonstrated. That a delivery platform good enough to migrate a bought book onto can be built on one small operation. That migration does not cost the book — at three to sixteen clients per asset (Node 02c), losing two clients is losing the asset. And that centralised terms still close deals against decentralised competitors bidding for the same owners.

Falsifiers, in the order they would appear. If the first migration runs past two quarters or loses an anchor client, we are the 19.9% population and the plan reverts to fewer and larger assets. If owners in our vertical refuse centralised terms, the offer has to be repriced in cash, which lifts the entry multiple and breaks the capital arithmetic in Node 02c. And if a platform already running AI-native delivery begins buying in financial services, the sequencing argument above expires — the capability gap we intend to build into is being closed by someone with a head start.

Part II · Node 05

Two companies

A dollar of vertical software revenue is worth three to eight dollars of managed-services revenue — which is the whole case for separating them. What the case does not survive is the assumption that the slot is empty: the horizontal version of the product already exists, and it is owned by the company that owns the PSA.

The question, and the criterion

One perimeter or two, and if two, from when. The principal's position is that they cannot be one: «you cannot cross a bulldog with a rhinoceros» — a private-equity game and a software vendor are different businesses with different risks and different multiples. The criterion is narrower than the intuition: separation is justified only if it raises founder cash-out by more than the cost of running two entities, and only once there is something to separate.

What this rests on, and what would break it

It rests on the services business existing at all — today the managed-services leg is seven people and one anchor client at 25 seats, and a channel that small cannot prove a product. And it rests on the compliance layer being genuinely separable from delivery, rather than being a set of reports only meaningful inside our own operating model.

Falsifiers. If an incumbent ships financial-services compliance evidence as a module, the vertical slot closes and the software entity has no case — the correct response then is to stop, not to differentiate. If eighteen months after the first acquisition there is still no customer outside the portfolio, the product is internal tooling and should be accounted for as such rather than carried as a second company in the plan. And if the software team can only be staffed by pulling people off delivery, the two-entity thesis is a resourcing fiction: one business cannot fund two at this scale.
