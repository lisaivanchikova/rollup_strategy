MSP after ChannelCon — where we are heading

A read-out from three days at ChannelCon 2026, San Diego, and what it settles about our direction inside managed services. Working detail, sources and open items sit in the full analysis.
Managed services is a fragmented industry with an unusually wide performance spread, and the spread is operational rather than structural — half the gross margin, half the utilisation and three and a half times the ticket load separate the bottom quartile from the leading group. That is what makes it worth consolidating: the distance between a weak provider and a strong one is made of systems, and systems can be installed. Our direction is thirteen small providers serving mid-market financial firms, standardised onto one delivery platform, toward $100M of enterprise value in five years.
1 · What the numbers look like

The industry runs at two speeds, and the gap is bigger than the margin difference between most whole sectors

Around 40,000 to 50,000 providers operate in the United States and none holds a meaningful share. The full-day financial masterclass put the peer-group data on screen, and it is the most useful thing we came back with.

The mechanics behind those rows are consistent. The bottom quartile pushes three and a half times the ticket volume through each engineer and gets a fifteen-hour first response for it, against forty-six minutes for the profitable cohort — efficiency and service quality fail together rather than trading off. Churn accounts for roughly three quarters of the growth gap. And the shrinking cohort spends 0.4% of revenue on winning work against 1.5% for the cohort that grows.

The independent index says the same from a different angle: investment-class providers have held 19%+ adjusted EBITDA for six consecutive years, and top-quartile firms earn about 2.5 times the EBITDA percentage of their median peers — attributed to operational maturity rather than to scale or market.

The practical read. The leading figures come from firms that are mostly small, so this is not about size. It is about dispatch discipline, utilisation, ticket deflection, collections and a real sales line — each of them a system that transfers between companies.

2 · Where inside MSP we play

Seven filters cut 40,000 providers to a pool in the low hundreds

An MSP runs another company's IT. Its «vertical» is the industry its clients are in — a financial-services provider serves banks, brokers and advisers; a healthcare one serves clinics and dental practices. Fifty-eight per cent of US providers now concentrate on at least one, against thirty-nine in 2021, and it pays: specialists price at $250–350 per seat against $100–120 and carry roughly 23% more margin.

The verticals inside MSP, and where each stands for us

Financial services and healthcare are the two most contested slices in the market — healthcare's vertical concentration moved from 18% to 31% in a year, financial services from 13% to 23%. We take financial services on room and on the transferability of what we already know from building software for crypto-trading firms; healthcare stays a reserve rather than a rejection. Worth being plain about it: the domain-knowledge advantage is real to us and has not yet been tested against this market.

Inside financial services the better slice is broker-dealers and registered advisers rather than banks. Banks carry the heavier compliance load, but their core systems resist automation and the slice is more consolidated, so there is less margin for us to add.

3 · How entry works, and where we stand

The positions in this market are set by what an entrant already owns, not by appetite for risk

The consolidators fall into distinct postures, and each has a hard precondition. Evergreen holds permanently on fund-scale capital and never sells. New Charter runs on a private-equity sponsor from launch. ITPartners+ standardises everything from day one — one team, one coat of paint — financed by $30M of private credit for 4% of equity. And the fourth posture, replacing an acquired operation outright and keeping only its clients, has no completed precedent in the market.

What decides the posture is integration capacity rather than deal supply. More than 70% of integrations miss the synergies modelled at close, and private-equity deals carrying more than two add-ons returned 19.9% against 23.1% for standalone buyouts. That capability is built across repeated deals, not bought with the first one.

So the sequence matters more than the position. Buy one provider at $3–4M of revenue and 5–6× adjusted EBITDA. Build the delivery platform on it and on the operation we already run. From the second deal onward, migration onto that platform is the integration — which is how an AI-native operating model gets executed at three deals a year instead of thirteen at once. A larger first asset is ruled out by arithmetic rather than preference: at $10M of revenue it costs $18M, sixty per cent of the budget for a quarter of the target. A platform of this kind is assembled, not bought.

4 · What happens next

The first acquisition settles four things that no further desk work can

The next milestone is the first asset under letter of intent at 5–6× adjusted EBITDA, with 70%+ recurring revenue, an assignability clause and no ninety-day cancellation right. That single transaction prices our client-acquisition cost, tests the integration claim, gives a lender a borrowing base, and shows whether small firms in this slice transact at 4–5× or nearer 6× — which is the assumption the whole margin arithmetic rests on. If a hundred conversations produce no letter of intent across two quarters, the entry model is wrong rather than the vertical.

Five things stay open, and they are listed with owners in the analysis. Delivery footprint — onshore, nearshore or offshore — remains unresolved and collides with the round-the-clock coverage this vertical expects. The size of the addressable pool is an estimate compounded from five judgements and is best settled by buying establishment data rather than by more analysis. The exit premium optimises at 40–60% vertical concentration, which is worth checking with the bankers who would eventually sell the group. Deal-flow terms with the peer-group advisor are live. And the software layer has a defensible vertical scope but no demand established outside our own portfolio.
