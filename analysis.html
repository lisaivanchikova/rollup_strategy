MSP roll-up — full analysis

Five levels, top down: the market, the filters that cut it to our slice, the entry forks, the go-to-market, and the decision. This is the working layer — the short document draws from it and links back here. Primary evidence is ChannelCon 2026: 36 recorded sessions and 100 photographed slides, 3–5 August, including the full-day masterclass «Financial Mastery for MSPs» run by MSP Ignite, Alternative Payments and Cytracom, whose quartile benchmarks carry most of Level 1. External figures are named at the point of use.
Level 1

The industry looks fragmented, but the gap between a good MSP and a bad one is enormous — and it is operational, which is why it can be bought

Roughly 40,000 to 50,000 managed service providers operate in the United States. No player holds a meaningful share. On the standard reading that makes this a fragmented industry — many ways to compete, small advantage available to each — and fragmented industries are usually poor places to consolidate, because there is nothing much to win by getting bigger.

The operating data says the opposite, and it is the most important thing in this analysis. The spread between the bottom quartile and the leading peer group is not a few points. It is the difference between a business that is dying and one that compounds.

The dispersion is not luck, and the presenters said so

Three of these rows explain most of the others. The bottom quartile pushes 3.6 times the ticket volume through each technician — and gets worse service out of it, not better, because the queue is what produces the fifteen-hour response. Efficiency and service quality do not trade off against each other here; they fail together. Churn accounts for roughly three quarters of the growth gap. And the shrinking cohort spends 0.4% of revenue on winning work against 1.5% for the cohort that grows — «the shrinking is a decision, not weather».

The same picture appears in the independent index: investment-class providers have posted 19%+ adjusted EBITDA for six consecutive years, and top-quartile firms earn about 2.5 times the EBITDA percentage of their median peers, attributed to operational maturity rather than scale or market. Service Leadership Index 2026

This is what makes the industry consolidatable, and it is a narrow claim. We are not betting that bigger MSPs are better — the top-quartile numbers above come from firms that are mostly small. We are betting that the distance between quartiles is made of things a competent operator can install: dispatch discipline, utilisation, ticket deflection, collections, a real sales line. Every one of those is a system, and systems transfer. If instead the gap were driven by market, client mix or geography, none of it would transfer and the whole thesis would collapse into buying revenue at a multiple.

Where that places the industry strategically

In the BCG advantage framing, an industry is characterised by two things: how many ways there are to compete, and how large an advantage is available to whoever competes well. MSP has many ways to compete — which is why it looks fragmented. But the exhibit above says the advantage available is large, not small.

The distinction matters because it dictates the move. In a genuinely fragmented industry the only lever is scale, and scale in services is weak. In a specialisation industry the lever is being systematically better at a defined thing for a defined customer — which is exactly what the vertical premium in Level 2 measures, and exactly what a roll-up can install repeatedly.

Nearly half of those transactions fold a smaller business into a larger platform. The named acquirers include Evergreen Services Group, New Charter Technologies, Logically, ConvergeOne, Magna5, ProArch, Ntiva and All Covered, with thirty to fifty regional private-equity-backed platforms operating below them. Evergreen alone completed 47 acquisitions in 2025 and has passed 100 in total.

Two consequences, and they point in opposite directions. The exit is real: there is a deep, funded buyer market for an assembled group, which is what makes a five-year plan finishable. And the entry is contested: the small firms we intend to buy cheaply are the same firms thirty to fifty other platforms are buying, which is the pressure that shows up as a pricing problem in Level 4.

What is not established at this level

Nothing here says an outsider can install those systems, or how fast. The quartile spread is a description of the industry, not evidence about us — and the outside record on serial acquisition is unfriendly, which Level 3 takes up directly. The spread also carries a survivorship caveat: the peer-group data comes from firms that joined a peer group, which is itself a marker of the kind of owner who measures things.

Level 2

Seven filters cut 40,000 providers to somewhere between 70 and 220 firms — and only the first two rest on published data

Each filter below removes a degree of freedom and states what it costs to remove it. They are applied in sequence, and the order matters: each cut runs against the slice the previous one left, so a filter applied too early removes firms that would have survived a later one.

The honest reading of that last column. Two filters are measured and five are judgement, and the five multiply. Four successive estimates of «about a third» compound into an answer with very wide error bars — the range 70 to 220 is an order of magnitude, not a count. What can be said with confidence is that the addressable pool is in the low hundreds, not the thousands, and that we need thirteen of them. That is a conversion of roughly one in ten of every firm that exists in our slice, which is the real measure of how narrow the strategy is.

Filter 1 · Specialise, and the choice of axis is not free

Two axes of specialisation were observed in the same room and they exclude each other. By client industry — learn one sector's language and compliance load, and «walk into any vertical because everyone there knows me». By service — one capability sold industry-agnostically, the working example being an operator who does nothing but database administration.

The economics decide it. Vertical specialists price at $250–350 per seat against $100–120 for generalists and carry roughly 23% more margin. The service-specialist route is not refuted so much as capped: the database-only operator had reached $750K of revenue after three decades — high margin, no growth engine, unable to win new clients. On the supply-side screen of 5 August, generalist MSPs score 5.10 and eliminate, on the largest available pool and the cheapest entry, because no moat and compressed pricing cannot be offset.

The constraint on the middle is stated flatly and it is a decision rule, not a preference: «I don't think the middle options — I'm not here, not there, undecided — are good or fit. It means we simply haven't done our homework.»

Filter 2 · Financial services, and within it the slice is narrower than «finance»

The screen of 5 August ran twenty client-vertical slices on acquirer-side criteria. Three passed: MSPs serving broker-dealers and RIAs (6.96), banks and credit unions (6.74), healthcare providers (6.56). The separation inside financial services is mechanical — banks carry the heavier compliance load but their core systems resist automation and the slice is more consolidated, so the margin we can add is smaller. Broker-dealers give almost the same regulatory moat with more compressible work.

What the vertical actually sells is an evidentiary obligation the client cannot meet alone: SEC Regulation S-P, FINRA Rules 3110 and 4370, FINRA 17a-4(f) on records, and the GLBA Safeguards Rule enforced across advisers and lenders. FINRA examinations and GLBA audits require documentation a generic provider cannot produce — which is why the barrier converts into price per seat rather than into a marketing claim.

Healthcare is third, and it loses on crowding rather than on quality. It is the largest specialist pool — 34% of MSPs serve it — and the most consolidated, rising from 18% of MSP vertical concentration in 2021 to 31% in 2022, with financial services second and moving the same way, 13% to 23%. Our two best verticals are the two most contested in the market. And the exit data does not separate them: the premium of 0.75–1.5 turns applies to regulated verticals as one category, with no ranking inside it.

Filter 3 · Concentration, and the ceiling nobody expected

Serving a vertical is not specialising in it. The transaction data attaches the premium to 40%+ of revenue in regulated verticals — and attaches a ceiling to it as well: the premium optimises in the 40–60% band, above which buyers begin discounting for customer concentration.

That cuts against the instinct to specialise as hard as possible. A pure-play single-vertical MSP is not the most valuable configuration, and at our asset size — three to sixteen clients — a single vertical is likely to be the entire book. The reconciliation is a live question for Level 4 rather than a settled one: it may mean the assembled group should hold a deliberate minority of adjacent revenue rather than driving the vertical to 100%.

Filter 4 · Client size, and the figure that travelled wrong

Mid-market: not enterprise, which means bidding against very large firms, and not SMB, whose decisions are «largely driven on cost». The mid-market outsources by preference and is willing to spend.

The range circulating internally has been 15–500 employees. The source said 50–500. The veteran gave it as fifty to five hundred; it was repeated back as fifteen within the same exchange and went uncorrected. The 15–50 band is precisely the price-driven SMB the same expert ruled out, so carrying it into the profile imports the segment we were warned against.

Filter 5 · States, and California as a mechanism rather than a warning

«Where should we operate» is three questions with three answers: where buyable assets are, where the demand sits, and where technicians sit. Merging them is how the choice gets made badly. This filter answers the first two; delivery remains open.

The warning received was that California has difficult legislation and few MSPs. Half is unsupported — California is a recognised cluster and on raw counts is not thin. The other half is supported, and the mechanism is more useful than the claim. California overtime triggers after eight hours in a day rather than forty in a week; a missed meal break above five hours costs an extra hour of pay; and a technician monitoring a server through a break converts that break into compensable time. A rational owner stops at four employees, and a firm that never hired has no transferable operation to buy.

The finding, stated properly: California does not lack MSPs, it lacks MSPs with employees — the only kind that can be acquired. Low asset supply is the output of the labour regime, not a separate fact about the state. And it collides with the vertical: financial services makes 7×24 coverage a precondition, which means night shifts, on-call rotations and monitored breaks — the three exposures California prices highest. The vertical and that state are the worst available pairing, which is why the anchor is New York and the expansion is Texas.

Filter 6 · Asset size — the band where private equity is not looking

$150–850K of EBITDA at a 13% median margin implies $1.2–6.5M of revenue. The upper bound is not a preference but a defence: «hardly anyone looks at companies below two or three million of enterprise value, and that is exactly our segment». Below that line entry runs at 4–5× against a platform range of 8–10×, and the spread is the return. The lower bound is staffing — a solo practice has nothing transferable, which is the same test California failed.

Filter 7 · Recurring share is not a filter, and it is worth saying so

Every target profile in circulation, including the one used by the most active comparable acquirer, specifies 60%+ contracted recurring revenue. The benchmark data says the leading peer group runs 76% recurring and the bottom quartile 62%.

A 60% threshold therefore removes almost nobody. It reads as a selective criterion and functions as a hygiene check. This matters in two ways: the target profile is less discriminating than it sounds, so it should not be relied on to do screening work; and the real differentiator in revenue quality is not the 60% line but the distance from it to 76%, together with contract term and assignability — where a 90-day cancellation clause is valued as month-to-month regardless of what the recurring share says.

What the cascade implies for the funnel we have to build

A pool in the low hundreds cannot be worked with broad marketing; it has to be enumerated by name. It also puts one figure from Level 4 in a different light: the advisor under discussion runs peer groups containing around 130 owners across 14–15 groups, which is roughly the size of the entire estimated pool. His network is not a lead source — at this scale it is plausibly a large fraction of the market itself, which is what the retainer is actually pricing, and what makes the terms worth restructuring rather than refusing.

What is not established at this level

Five of seven filters are estimates, and the compounded range is wide enough that the pool could be seventy firms or three hundred. Hardening it is a data purchase, not an analysis task — establishment counts by sector, state and employee band, cross-referenced against provider directories. It is the cheapest open item in the programme and it gates the funnel in Level 4. The concentration ceiling from Filter 3 is also unresolved, and it is the one filter that argues against the strategy rather than for it.
